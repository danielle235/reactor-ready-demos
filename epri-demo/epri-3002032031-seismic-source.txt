ABSTRACT
This report evaluates the challenges of applying the Licens­
ing Modernization Project (LMP) risk-informed framework 
to seismic design for advanced reactors. While LMP aims to 
provide flexibility and cost efficiency through performance-
based criteria, its frequency-consequence (F-C) target of 
5E-7/yr for high-consequence Beyond Design Basis Events 
(BDBEs) can lead to overly conservative seismic require­
ments for structural design. Analyses show that meeting 
this target would require increasing design-basis ground 
motions by up to three times current U.S. Nuclear Regula­
tory Commission (USNRC) criteria, significantly raising costs 
without proportional safety benefits.
Comparative studies indicate that the incremental reduction 
in seismic risk achieved by these higher design demands is 
negligible compared to societal risks from widespread build­
ing collapses during earthquakes. Current USNRC seismic 
criteria already provide adequate protection under the 
Atomic Energy Act and align with safety policy goals. The 
report recommends a balanced approach that maintains 
safety objectives while avoiding unnecessary escalation of 
design demands, supporting practical implementation of 
risk-informed principles for advanced reactor deployment.
BACKGROUND
The Licensing Modernization Project (LMP) framework, 
described in Nuclear Energy Institute (NEI) Technical Report 
18-04 [1], outlines a process for Technology-Inclusive (TI) 
Risk-Informed Performance-Based (RIPB) design of non-light 
water reactors (NLWRs). The U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Com­
mission (USNRC) endorsed NEI 18-04 in Regulatory Guide 
(RG) 1.233 [2] with clarifications. The hallmark of RIPB is to 
allow developing design criteria for structures, systems, and 
components (SSCs) commensurate with their significance 
to ensuring that the overall plant design satisfies regulatory 
safety objectives, rather than specifying prescriptive design 
rules. The design flexibility, anticipated cost optimization 
benefits, and TI nature of RIPB design concepts in NEI 18-04 
elicited significant interest among the advanced reactor 
industry stakeholders. 
Following that, NEI 21-07 [3] provided guidance for develop­
ing standardized sections of the Safety Analysis Report (SAR) 
tailored for advanced reactor applicants who adopt NEI 18-04, 
with the aim of facilitating the preparation of the SAR by the

The TI RIPB guidance in NEI 18-04 [1] is based on three 
mainstays:
•	
Satisfying regulatory acceptance criteria.
•	
Risk significance-based safety classification.
•	
Confirming Defense in Depth (DiD) adequacy. 
NEI 18-04 [1] guidance for risk significance-based safety clas­
sification is based on the consequences and annual frequen­
cy – from a probabilistic risk assessment (PRA) model – of 
event sequence families, which are referred to as licensing-
basis events (LBEs). This guidance was developed with a 
focus on internal events PRA models and their typical out­
comes. It presents unanticipated challenges when applied 
to external events, especially seismic hazard that involves 
significantly higher uncertainty in its state of knowledge. 
EPRI 3002029295 [5] identified several of these challenges. 
OBJECTIVES
The objectives of this report are to investigate the applica­
bility Beyond Design Basis Events (BDBEs) to RIPB seismic 
design of the 5E-7/yr frequency target in NEI 18-04 for high-
consequence BDBEs, assess its implications on the design 
criteria, and present a recommended path for establishing 
seismic design basis ground motions and acceptance criteria.
NEI 18-04 RISK-INFORMED 
CLASSIFICATION OF LICENSING 
BASIS EVENTS
NEI 18-04 presents two sets of acceptance criteria: PRA-
based and deterministic.
PRA-based Acceptance Criteria
•	
The cumulative risk metrics from all the LBEs should be 
less than the specified thresholds for site boundary dose, 
average individual early fatality, and average individual 
latent cancer fatalities. These cumulative metrics are 
meant to be from all hazards and plant operating states.
Deterministic Acceptance Criteria
•	
LBEs identified as design basis accidents (DBAs) must 
meet the Title 10 of the Code of Federal Regulations 
(10CFR) 50.34 dose limit [6].
•	
DiD adequacy must be demonstrated.
Risk significance-based classification of LBEs and functions of 
SSCs is performed using a tool called the frequency-conse­
quence (F-C) chart. Figure 1 shows the F-C chart, as derived 
from NEI 18-04 [1], which shows the F-C target line and fre­
quency ranges for classifying LBEs as Anticipated Operational 
Occurrences (AOOs), Design Basis Events (DBEs), or BDBEs. 
An LBE is considered within the F-C target when a point 
defined by the upper 95th percentile uncertainty on both the 
LBE frequency and dose consequence is below the F-C target.

The USNRC is in the process of developing a regulatory 
guide for TI RIPB seismic design of commercial nuclear 
plants intended for use with advanced reactors. The current 
draft guide (DG)-1410 [7] identifies three licensing pathway

•

•

•

A proposed methodology to follow Option 3 is being 
developed by Argonne National Laboratory (ANL) in ANL/
NSE-24/42. This document is under preparation; the lat­
est draft was presented to USNRC staff and the public in 
ML25237A182 [9]. The draft methodology recommends, 
among other things, establishing a high-consequence seis­
mic BDBE acceptance criterion using a limited SPRA. The 
proposed acceptance criterion corresponds to a specified 
minimum margin or maximum conditional probability of 
dose release at a sufficiently high hazard level above the 
ground motion response spectrum (GMRS) as defined in 
RG 1.208 [10]. The seismic margin may be defined by the 
high confidence low probability of failure (HCLPF) ground 
motion capability or by some other parameter, such as the 
ground motion at a conditional failure probability of 0.05.
LBEs that register within 1% of the F-C target are consid­
ered risk-significant. The F-C chart is not an acceptance 
criterion, as stated in NEI 18-04 [1] and RG 1.233 [2]. It is a 
means to assess a range of events to determine their risk 
significance, support SSC classification, determine special 
treatment requirements, identify appropriate programmatic 
controls, and confirm the adequacy of DiD strategies. It can 
serve as a guide for improving the design and as a tool for 
understanding the risk consequences of design changes. 
NEI 18-04 refers to “keeping all LBEs within the F-C target” 
as a “design objective.” Meanwhile, EPRI 3002029295 [5] 
demonstrates that the regulatory acceptance criteria for 
cumulative risk and DBA dose can be achieved with several 
LBEs exceeding the F-C target. However, many advanced 
reactor developers interpret the NEI 18-04 statement as a 
goal for their designs to achieve, thus setting it effectively 
as an acceptance criterion. Moreover, despite not strictly 
being an acceptance criterion, exceeding the F-C target for 
high-consequence BDBEs1 has implications in LMP that can 
be costly to the design, such as classifying SSCs.
Implications for RIPB Seismic Design
Since the F-C chart compares the PRA risk insights on an 
LBE basis, high-consequence LBEs have much lower target 
frequencies. For LBEs with dose consequences of 750 rem 
or higher at the exclusion area boundary (EAB), the F-C 
target is 5E-7/yr (Figure 1).
Seismic-induced LBEs of such high consequence can be 
dominated by integrity failures of major reactor structures, 
such as the reactor pressure vessel, reactor building, and/
or containment if required in the design. These LBEs for 
external hazards, in which a major structure loses structural 
integrity with no redundancy and only partially feasible 
release mitigation features, often consist of a singleton or 
near-singleton event sequence, which is not common in 
internal events PRA. Consequently, meeting the 5E-7/yr F-C 
target often translates to seismic design where the annual 
frequency of losing sufficient structural integrity resulting in 
a high-consequence dose release is less than 5E-7/yr with 
95% confidence for all such major structures. These

The MCER is determined in ASCE/SEI 7-22 [21] on a site-
specific basis such that the expected probability of col­
lapse for Category I & II structures is 1% in 50 years, which 
equates to an annual frequency of about 2E-4/yr. This 
collapse probability is calculated using the seismic hazard 
at each site from the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS), a mean 
fragility curve defined by Pf at the MCER, and a logarithmic 
standard deviation βc of 0.6, which is judged to represent 
uncertainty in commercial construction. The 50-year col­
lapse probabilities for Risk Category III and IV structures 
do not have explicit targets in ASCE/SEI 7-22 [21]. They can 
be estimated based on the study performed in Luco (2020) 
[22], which showed structure designs anchored to a Pf 
equal to 1% at the MCER and βc of 0.6 results in annual fre­
quencies of collapse 45% lower than those of Risk Category 
I & II structures based on convolving with seismic hazard 
at thirty-four site locations, two alternative site profiles, 
and three spectral frequencies. Using log-log interpolation 
between Pf of 1% and 10% to the corresponding values for 
Risk Category III and IV, the expected annual frequencies of 
collapse of these structures are estimated to be 1.7E-4/yr 
and 1.4E-4/yr, respectively. Accordingly, the collapse risk of 
current code-confirming commercial structures ranges from 
1.4E-4/yr to 2.0E-4/yr for all structure risk categories.
Compared to the annual frequencies of current convention­
ally designed NPP major safety-related structures failing to 
perform their RSFs (Table 1), the expected annual collapse 
risk of commercial structures is about 30 to 150 times 
higher, depending on the site, design reliability, and risk 
category. This relative level of risk from safety-related NPP 
structures is currently accepted by the USNRC, implying 
that the seismic performance of current nuclear design 
criteria provides “adequate protection” under the Atomic 
Energy Act [23], aligns with USNRC qualitative safety goals, 
and does not constitute “a significant addition to other 
societal risk.”
Infrastructure built to commercial design codes would expe­
rience widespread damage in strong earthquakes compared 
to those designed to conventional safety-related nuclear 
standards. Current infrastructure was predominantly built 
over several decades, using seismic design codes and con­
struction practices that do not reflect current knowledge 
and lessons learned about building vulnerabilities in mod­
ern commercial codes. This existing building stock would, on 
average, perform worse than predicted for current code-
conforming structures. Compared to the relative risk of 
widespread building collapses in an urban area following an 
earthquake and its societal impact, revising seismic design 
criteria for NPP structures to further decrease the risk of 
severe accidents may be prioritizing the wrong issue. For 
example, the United Nations concluded that “no adverse 
health effects among Fukushima residents have been docu­
mented that are directly attributable to radiation exposure 
from the [Fukushima Dai-Ichi Nuclear Power Station] acci­
dent” [20]. Meanwhile, the 2011 Great Tohoku earthquake 
resulted in the loss of more than ten thousand lives.
The following two subsections investigate the expected 
relative societal impact by comparing estimates of the an­
nual frequencies of a nuclear release due to a major struc­
ture failure that directly results in a high-consequence dose 
release compared to that of building collapse or severe 
damage in the cases of current code-conforming buildings 
and existing U.S. building stocks, respectively.

Societal Impact Relative to Current U.S. 
Building Stock
Estimating conditional probabilities of structure collapse at 
1.67 times the GMRS for current U.S. building stock de­
pends on several factors that are beyond the scope of this 
report, including the building inventory in each area, the 
vintage of each structure, its structural system, the build­
ing code in effect at the time of the design, its conformance 
to the code, and potential seismic upgrades or structural 
degradation with aging. While it is not practical to estimate 
probabilities analogous to those in Table 4, it is reasonable 
to assert that the corresponding probabilities for an aver­
age structure in the current U.S. building stock are higher 
than those for modern code-conforming structures in 
Table 4. This section describes a reasonable basis to charac­
terize the societal risk of the current U.S. building stock on 
an aggregate level.

The risk computations underpinning FEMA P-366 are 
performed in FEMA software Hazus Version 6.0. Detailed 
descriptions of the risk assessment methods, data, as­
sumptions, and limitations of the Hazus computations are 
provided in the “Hazus Earthquake Model User Guidance” 
[26] and the “Hazus Earthquake Model Technical Manual” 
[27]. Hazus conducts loss computations based on (1) seismic 
hazard, which can be user- or database-input, for example 
the USGS hazard maps averaged per census tract; (2) build­
ing stock data, such as types of construction, footprint, 
number of floors, use, occupancy, age, and potential retro­

Engineers (USACE)’s National Structure Inventory (NSI) 2022 
[28] and the Department of Homeland Security (DHS)’s 
Homeland Infrastructure Foundation-Level Data (HIFLD) 
[29]; (3) building and component vulnerability and damage 
functions (similar to fragility functions) from Hazus database 
for prototypical construction types and vintages; (4) typical 
building prototype contents per area or floor based on its 
type, use, and occupancy; and (5) repair and replacement 
cost values (2022 costs were used for FEMA P 366) aggre­
gated by Gordian [30]. Hazus can run both deterministic loss 
estimation analyses (for a single-earthquake scenario) and 
probabilistic loss estimation analyses (expected annualized 
values). FEMA P-366 presents the results of a probabilistic 
seismic risk analysis for the entire United States, broken 
down at national, regional, state, and county levels.

nationwide AEL is in California, Oregon, and Washington.

CONCLUSIONS
Based on the observations and discussion above, the fol­
lowing path is recommend for RIPB seismic design of SSCs 
in advanced reactors, consistent with LMP:
•	
For safety-related SSCs, the DBHL earthquake need not 
exceed the GMRS defined according to RG 1.208 [10]. 
•	
High-consequence seismic BDBEs are allowed to exceed 
the F-C target provided that:
	–
The union of these BDBEs demonstrates a seismic 
margin (HCLPF capacity) equal to at least 1.67 
times the GMRS; and
	–
The cumulative risk metrics acceptance criteria or 
USNRC-endorsed surrogates (such as being devel­
oped in ANL/NSE-24/42 [9]) are met. 
•	
The seismic design basis for non-safety related SSCs may 
follow commercial design codes and standards with spe­
cial treatment as warranted to risk-significant SSCs.
These recommendations are consistent with the LMP 
process outlined in NEI 18-04 while explicitly deprioritizing 
staying within the F-C target for seismic BDBEs as long as 
the cumulative risk criteria are met.
While LMP aims to provide flexibility and cost efficiency 
through performance-based criteria, its F-C target of 5E 7/yr 
for high-consequence BDBEs can lead to overly conservative 
seismic design requirements. The incremental reduction 
in seismic risk achieved by these higher design demands is 
negligible compared to societal risks from widespread build­
ing collapses during earthquakes. Current USNRC seismic 
criteria already provide adequate protection under the 
Atomic Energy Act and align with safety policy goals. The 
recommended approach maintains safety objectives while 
avoiding unnecessary escalation of design demands, sup­
porting practical implementation of risk-informed principles 
for advanced reactor design.

APPENDIX A – HAZARD CURVE 
SLOPE
Seismic hazard curves typically depict the relationship 
between a ground motion intensity parameter on the x-
axis and the annual frequency of exceedance (AFE) on the 
y-axis. The most common ground motion intensity param­
eter is the peak ground acceleration (PGA), followed by the 
spectral acceleration response of a single-degree-of-free­
dom oscillator with a specified frequency. Other parameters 
include the peak ground velocity (PGV) and cumulative 
absolute velocity (CAV). Without loss of generality, this 
discussion uses the PGA. 
It is common in seismic risk computations to idealize seismic 
hazard curves using a power law, which produces a straight-
line relationship in log-log space, and fragilities using lognor­
mal functions. Even though actual seismic hazard curves are 
curvilinear, the power law idealization is a valid idealization 
because: (1) for typical seismic hazard and fragility curves, 
the risk convolution integral results in the vast majority of 
seismic risk resulting from ground motion intensity bins that 
span a single AFE decade or less, and (2) and the typical 
seismic hazard curvature is mild such that it can accurately 
be represented as piecewise linear over each AFE decade.
